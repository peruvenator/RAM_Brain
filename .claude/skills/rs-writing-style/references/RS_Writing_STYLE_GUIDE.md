# Return Stacked Style Guide
## For Perfect Style Transfer Across All Content Types

> **ABSOLUTE RULE: NO EM DASHES.** Never use em dashes (—) in any Return Stacked content. Restructure sentences using commas, colons, semicolons, parentheses, or separate sentences instead. This applies to all artifact types without exception.

# 1. Voice and Identity

## Who Is Speaking

The Return Stacked voice is a **thoughtful institutional practitioner** -- someone who has spent decades reading the papers, running simulations, managing portfolios, and now shares what they have learned. The author is not a journalist, not a salesperson, not a pure academic. The voice sits at the intersection of all three: rigorous enough to cite Koijen et al., practical enough to tell an advisor exactly which bonds to sell, and human enough to acknowledge that investors are emotional creatures.

Write like a sharp colleague explaining something over coffee -- knowledgeable, direct, occasionally witty, never flippant. Educate, don't sell. The reader should leave smarter. Trust that good thinking creates its own gravity.

The single most important tonal quality: **earned confidence.** The authors sound like people who have done the work. They hedge on predictions but never on principles.

## What the Voice Is Not

- **Not academic** -- avoid dense, jargon-heavy prose that reads like a peer-reviewed submission
- **Not salesy** -- never pressure, hype, or over-promise
- **Not bland** -- vanilla "financial institution voice" is the enemy; have a point of view
- **Not casual** to the point of undermining credibility

## Core Writing Principles

- **Lead with honesty, not salesmanship.** The most compelling framing is often "here's the answer, and here's why you probably shouldn't use it." This earned-trust posture is a brand differentiator.
- **Every paragraph earns its place.** Cut anything that exists only to sound thorough.
- **Acknowledge the human element directly.** The best technical analysis loses if it ignores behavioral reality. Weave in practitioner experience: quarterly reviews, client phone calls, line-item scrutiny. E.g. "The optimizer doesn't know about client phone calls. It doesn't model the very human tendency to abandon a strategy at the worst possible time."
- **Ground in real decisions.** Readers are not reading for theory alone -- they are trying to decide whether and how to implement something. Ground the writing in the decisions they actually face: what to allocate, how much, and how to defend that choice in a quarterly review.

## Pronoun Rules

- **Default to first person plural ("we/our").** This is the signature pronoun choice. It creates shared inquiry between author and reader.
  - "We believe the answer is a resounding 'no.'"
  - "In this post we strived to provide the intuition -- and math -- as to why volatility is a critically important ingredient."
- **Use second person ("you/your") when addressing the advisor's practice** or walking through implementation examples.
  - "If you wanted to stack the alternative strategy on top of your existing portfolio..."
- **First person singular ("I") is almost never used.** Even single-author perspectives default to "we." The only "I" appears in hypothetical investor voices: "Have I missed the bus on return stacking?"
- **Third person for market participants generally:** "Investors often struggle to hold onto diversifiers."

## Tone Calibration

| Dimension | Position |
|---|---|
| Formal ↔ Conversational | 65% formal -- professional but accessible, occasional contractions ("it's", "doesn't") |
| Confident ↔ Hedged | Confident on concepts and principles; hedged on all forward-looking performance claims |
| Authoritative ↔ Collaborative | Authoritative on framework, collaborative on application |
| Passionate ↔ Measured | Cool-to-warm; controlled passion for democratization of institutional strategies, measured calm for risk |

## Confidence Rules

**Make bold claims about:**
- The structural logic of return stacking (concepts, mechanics, math)
- Historical evidence ("trend following produced positive returns in eight out of ten worst drawdowns")
- Behavioral realities ("investors struggle to hold onto diversifiers")
- What is NOT true ("rebalance drag is not a real effect"; "leverage is not inherently dangerous")

**Hedge with "may," "can," "potentially," "seeks to" when discussing:**
- Future performance expectations
- Forward-looking behavior of any specific strategy
- Any claim that could be interpreted as a guarantee
- Product-specific outcomes

**Drop all hedging for:**
- Mathematical truths ("Volatility pushes the compound rate further from the average")
- Historical facts ("86.6% of large-cap funds have underperformed the S&P 500")
- Definitional statements ("Carry is the expected return assuming no change in price")

## Personality Markers

- **Intellectual playfulness in service of explanation** -- never humor for humor's sake. Wit is structural and conceptual: clever acronyms (LICE), mystery reveals, theatrical framing (Act I / Act II), vivid metaphors.
- **No jokes, no self-deprecation, no unrelated personal asides.** Exception: brief reader-reassurance in math-heavy sections is permitted and characteristic ("don't worry, it's pretty simple"; "If you're allergic to math, we'd recommend simply skipping to the example below").
- **Gravitas around risk.** Playfulness recedes when discussing leverage risk, path-dependency, or permanent capital impairment.
- **The "advisor as hero" framing.** The financial advisor is always the protagonist who will bring these ideas to clients. The advisor is never criticized or belittled. They are empowered.

# 2. Argumentation Architecture

## The Master Persuasion Pattern

Every article follows this architecture:

1. **Hook with tension.** Open by promising the answer to the obvious question, then immediately complicate it. Start with a provocative observation, counterintuitive insight, or a question the reader didn't know they had. Never open with "In this post, we will discuss..."
2. **Start from where the reader stands.** Ground in what the advisor already believes (60/40 works, diversification matters, leverage is scary).
3. **Introduce friction gently.** Show the conventional approach has a specific, named problem (tracking error double-whammy, line-item bias, variance drain, the subtraction problem).
4. **Reframe the problem as structural, not personal.** The reader is not wrong; the toolbox was incomplete.
5. **Show, don't tell.** Every claim is supported by a figure, table, or simulation. Introduce a figure at the moment the reader needs it to understand the argument. Never dump data without context.
6. **Name the solution as evolution, not revolution.** Return stacking is "an old idea reborn," "portable alpha for everyone" -- an enhancement, not a replacement.
7. **Soften all forward-looking claims.**
8. **Address objections before the reader raises them.**
9. **Close with actionable takeaways** (standard blog posts) **or a principle** (deep-dive pillar content). Standard posts get Key Takeaways bullets. Deep-dive posts synthesize a heuristic the reader can internalize, not a specific allocation they should copy.

**Narrative arc over listicle.** The post should read as an argument that builds, not a collection of tips. Each section should follow logically from the last. Use backward references to recap ("So far we've looked at each diversifier in isolation") and forward references to preview direction ("Before we get to stacking, let's see what happens without any constraints"). Sections should feel like a continuous argument, not standalone modules.

## The "Reframe" Move

This is the core rhetorical engine. Nearly every article performs at least one conventional wisdom reversal using the **"Not X, but Y"** construction:

- "Leverage is a tool, not a villain."
- "It's not about doubling down on fear. It's about layering in durability."
- "Not as a silver bullet, but as an evolution."
- "The real question is not 'Is leverage bad?' but rather 'How is leverage being used?'"
- "Stacking is not a forecast. It's a framework."

The reframe always begins by **validating the conventional view** ("This view is not entirely unwarranted"), then introduces nuance. Never combative.

## The "And Not Or" / "Yes And" Move

A sibling pattern to the "Not X, but Y" reframe, equally frequent and arguably more central to the brand's persuasion. This move **dissolves false dichotomies** in portfolio construction:

- "We advocate for saying '*and*' not '*or*'. Return stacking eliminates the need to make this choice."
- "We no longer must make the decision between keeping cash and investing. We can do both."
- Section title: **"The 'Yes And' Approach: Return Stacking"**

Where the "Not X, but Y" reframe corrects a misconception, the "And Not Or" move removes a perceived tradeoff. Use it whenever the conventional framing forces investors to choose between two good things (stocks vs. alternatives, cash vs. returns, diversification vs. benchmark tracking).

## Counterargument Handling: Steelman-Concede-Recontextualize

Never dismiss objections. Follow this pattern:

1. **Name the objection explicitly**, often as a direct quote or rhetorical question:
   - "Isn't this supposed to hedge my equity risk? Why isn't it working now?"
2. **Concede real validity:**
   - "It's a fair question. And the answer, like with most things in investing, is nuanced."
3. **Recontextualize:**
   - "This concern is best framed not as a flaw in managed futures, but as a form of myopia."

Naming cognitive biases (line-item bias, product myopia, leverage myopia) transforms a rational-seeming objection into a documented psychological pitfall.

## Academic-to-Practical Bridge

Restate the academic finding, then immediately pivot to portfolio implications:

- **Bridging phrases:** "This means...", "In other words...", "Put another way...", "What does this mean for investors..."
- **Dollar-denominated framing** over abstract percentages: "a $100 position in a U.S. Treasury futures contract may only require $5 to $10 in margin"
- **Concrete portfolio arithmetic:** "selling 6% stocks and 4% bonds to make room for a 10% position"

## Progressive Disclosure

Follow strict **concrete-first, math-later** layering:

1. Plain-language statement of the core idea
2. Simple numerical example with a small table
3. Extension to realistic scenarios
4. Full mathematical derivation (only after intuition is established)
5. Conclusion restating the intuitive version, now enriched by the math

### The Formula-to-Punchline Pattern

When math appears, follow this specific sequence:

1. Introduce the formula with named variables
2. Provide a concrete numeric example immediately after
3. Simplify the formula to a "punchline" result (e.g., "E[R_w] = E[R_p]")
4. Restate the punchline in plain language ("In other words, the cash drag is completely offset")

A brief conversational aside ("don't worry, it's pretty simple") is permitted and characteristic when transitioning into algebraic content.

## Data Presentation Rhetoric

- **Always precede data with a framing statement** telling the reader what to look for: "As the chart clearly illustrates..." / "What really grabs our attention is..."
- **Contextualize every number:** "Only 41 of 3,220 outperformed the S&P 500."
- **Juxtapose small input changes with large outcome changes:** "a seemingly small increase in withdrawal rate has a profound impact on longevity risk."

# 3. Sentence-Level Craft

## Rhythm

Bimodal: medium-to-long expository sentences (25-45 words) carry the analytical weight, punctuated by short declarative sentences (5-15 words) at moments of rhetorical emphasis.

- Long: "By incorporating uncorrelated alternative investments and asset classes, portfolios can generate steadier returns across various economic regimes, reducing vulnerability during periods of market stress."
- Short (for punch): "Sound familiar?" / "The picture changes dramatically." / "Return stacking offers an alternative."

Short sentences appear only at structural pivot points, conclusions, and reframes. Never mid-paragraph for routine exposition.

**Short paragraph clusters create rhythm.** A sequence of 2-3 short paragraphs (1-2 sentences each) can build momentum before a longer explanatory paragraph delivers the payoff. E.g. "We have the answer. We ran the optimizer. And we're going to share it with you. But first, we need to talk about why you probably shouldn't use it."

## The Signature Sentence Pattern

**"By [gerund], [subject] can [verb]"** -- this is the single most distinctive syntactic fingerprint:

- "By incorporating uncorrelated alternative investments, portfolios can generate steadier returns."
- "By separating alpha from beta, we can seek to develop a structural edge."
- "By embedding managed futures into a single, return stacked investment, advisors can reduce friction."
- "By stacking managed futures on top of equities, the stacked exposure added diversification."

Use this construction 2-4 times per article.

## Sentence Openers (by frequency)

1. **Subject-verb (~50%):** "Leverage magnifies exposure." / "Investors often fixate on individual components."
2. **Prepositional/temporal phrase (~20%):** "In the realm of investment management..." / "For decades, sophisticated institutional investors..."
3. **Participial/gerund (~15%):** "By focusing on futures..." / "Recognizing the urgency..."
4. **Transitional (~15%):** "However, leverage itself is neither good nor bad..." / "Crucially, it often lagged..."

**Never** begin sentences with "I". Coordinating conjunctions ("And", "But") at sentence start appear occasionally in all content types but should be used sparingly -- no more than 1-2 per article.

## Punctuation and Mechanics

- **Oxford comma:** Always.
- **Em dashes: NEVER USE.** Em dashes (—) are prohibited in all Return Stacked content. Restructure using commas, colons, semicolons, parentheses, or separate sentences. No exceptions.
- **Semicolons:** Rare in blog posts; occasional in formal research. Prefer breaking into separate sentences.
- **Colons:** Common for introducing lists, definitions, explanations.
- **Parentheticals:** 2-4 per article for technical clarifications: "(e.g., stocks and bonds)", "(a '100/100' fund)".
- **Contractions:** Fine in blog posts and advisor guides. They contribute to the conversational register. Avoid in whitepapers and product briefs.
- **Italics:** For emphasis on key terms and conceptual contrast: "*volatility drag*", "*None of the above.*" Use italic lead-ins (subtitles) under the main title to frame a post's purpose.
- **Bold:** For key terms being defined, acronym introductions, key conclusions within a paragraph, figure labels, and lead-in phrases in bullet lists. **Do not bold for decoration or general emphasis** -- reserve it for phrases the reader should remember. In HTML context, bold is styled navy (#2A3F5B).
- **Numbers:** Spell out numbers under ten; numerals for 10+. Always numerals with units, percentages, and financial figures.

## Hedging Language (Systematic)

These terms are compliance-mandated and appear in nearly every paragraph of forward-looking content:

| Term | Usage |
|---|---|
| "may" / "can" | "Return stacking *may* help enhance long-term growth rates" |
| "seeks to" / "aims to" | "we *seek to* provide investors with the building blocks" |
| "potentially" | "potentially diversifying alternative return streams" |
| "historically" | "managed futures *have historically* performed well during equity drawdowns" |
| "tends to" | "carry *tends to* be rewarded over time" |
| "we believe" | Used to mark firm-level opinion: "We believe the answer is a resounding 'no.'" |

## Transition Phrases

- Between sentences: "However," / "This means..." / "Crucially," / "Put simply," / "In other words,"
- Between paragraphs: Rhetorical questions ("But how should advisors think about this?") or "Let's" constructions ("Let's explore...")
- Forward references: "In the following section, we will explore..."

## Active vs. Passive Voice

~75-80% active. Passive appears for:
- Process descriptions: "The freed-up capital could then be invested in hedge funds."
- De-emphasized agent (compliance-safe): "It is said that the only free lunch in investing is diversification."

## Characteristic Verb Selections

| Context | Preferred Verbs |
|---|---|
| Return stacking mechanics | layer, stack, overlay, embed, integrate, incorporate |
| Positive outcomes | unlock, harness, free up, capture, harvest |
| Negative framing | dilute, carve out, erode, amplify, exacerbate |
| Investor behavior | struggle, abandon, fixate, grapple |

## Prose Over Bullets in Body Text

**Prefer prose over bullet points in the article body.** The narrative voice is built on flowing paragraphs, not bulleted lists. When breaking down categories or options (e.g. different allocation approaches), use **bold-labeled prose paragraphs** rather than bullet lists. Each option gets a bold heading followed by 2-4 sentences of explanation in full prose:

> **Equal Weight:** The simplest approach: allocate equally across your chosen diversifiers. It's easy to explain, easy to implement, and historically has produced solid results.

Bullet points are acceptable in: methodology sections, data source listings, widget descriptions, Key Takeaways sections, and any context where scannable reference material is more useful than narrative flow.

## Inline Statistics

Numbers and statistics are introduced inline, woven into sentences rather than set apart: "roughly 2-3% annualized tracking error is the upper bound" or "Gold, with an annualized volatility of 16%, hits the 2% tracking error threshold at roughly a 12% stack size."

**Formulas belong in figures, captions, and methodology sections** -- not in the body prose. The prose should explain what a formula means and why it matters, not reproduce the math. (Exception: the formula-to-punchline pattern in dedicated technical/methodology sections -- see Progressive Disclosure.)

## Characteristic Adjectives

"diversifying" (return streams), "capital efficient," "uncorrelated," "complementary," "thoughtful/thoughtfully" (qualifying leverage or portfolio construction), "compelling," "meaningful," "core" (portfolio), "structural" (edge, alpha).

## Metaphor Rules

- Low-to-moderate density. The writing is analytical, not literary.
- Metaphors **land punches but don't carry paragraphs.**
- Introduce the physical/vivid analogy **BEFORE** the technical concept, not after.
- Signature recurring images: "at the scene of the crime" (leverage disasters), "building blocks" (products), "free lunch" (diversification), "make room for" (alternatives).
- **Never use:** sports metaphors, war metaphors, or overly casual metaphors.

## Numerical Formatting

- **Percentages:** Always with %, no space: "60%", "3.1%", "-37.0%"
- **Dollar amounts:** "$1.00" (with cents for the core concept), "$100 million" (spelled out, not "$100M")
- **Portfolio notation:** Slash notation: "60/40", "100/100", "50/30/20"
- **Correlations:** Two decimal places: "0.12", "-0.15"
- **Sharpe ratios:** Capitalized proper noun: "the Sharpe ratio"

## Academic Citation Style

- **In blog posts:** Inline hyperlinks to other RS articles; superscript footnotes for external academic sources
- **In academic reviews:** (Author, Year) or (Author et al., Year) inline
- **In whitepapers:** Formal parenthetical citations with References section: "(Asness, Moskowitz, and Pedersen 2013)"
- **Attribution language:** "the authors found that...", "the study demonstrates that...", "[Author] argued that..."

## Standard Disclaimers (Required)

Every figure source block must include the applicable elements below. Note: attribution format differs by authorship.

**Newfound Research-authored content:**
- "Source: [Data Provider]. Calculations by Newfound Research."

**ReSolve Asset Management-authored content:**
- "Source: ReSolve Asset Management Inc., [Data Providers]. Analysis by ReSolve Asset Management SEZC (Cayman)."
- Includes inline index definitions and date ranges within the caption.

**Common to all:**
- "Returns are gross of all fees, taxes, and transaction costs."
- "Performance assumes the reinvestment of all distributions."
- "Past performance is not indicative of future results."
- "You cannot invest in an index." (or "Investors cannot invest directly in an index.")
- "For illustrative purposes only."

# 5. Document Structure by Artifact Type

## Blog Posts (1,500-3,000 words; research-style: 3,000-6,000+)

**Metadata Block:** Most blog posts begin with a standardized header:
```
### Overview
[1-3 sentence summary of the article]
### Key Topics
[comma-separated keyword list]
```

**Opening:** Problem-statement-first. Identify a tension, misconception, or challenge within the first 2-4 sentences. Purely technical/educational articles may open with a definitional framing instead ("Many financial plans are built off the backbone of capital market assumptions..."), but this is the exception.

**Flow:** Problem --> Concept Introduction --> Evidence/Data (with figures) --> Practical Implication --> Conclusion --> Key Takeaways (bulleted, with bold lead-ins)

**Headers:** Title Case, 6-10 sections for standard posts. Longer research-style posts (3,000+ words) naturally scale to 15-20+ subsections. Mix of statements ("The Defensive Use of Leverage") and framings with colons ("Case Study: Comparing Reckless vs. Defensive Leverage"). **Headers should signal what the reader will learn, not just label topics.** A skimming reader should be able to learn the article's argument from headers alone.

**Paragraphs:** 2-4 sentences. Single-sentence paragraphs for emphasis or transitions between math and prose.

**First-mention linking:** Link the first mention of each core concept to its canonical RS article: "[managed futures](link)", "[portable alpha](link)", "[return stacking](link)".

**Figures:** 1-3, always with full source attribution blocks.

**Close:** 3-part pattern: (1) Summary restatement, (2) Forward-looking statement, (3) Key Takeaways as bullets with bold lead-ins. Soft CTA: "Connect with one of our Advisor Solution Representatives."

**Cross-linking:** Heavily link to other RS articles. Key concepts become hyperlinks: "[portable alpha](link)", "[return stacking](link)".

## Academic Reviews (800-1,100 words)

**Opening:** Bibliographic header (Authors, Journal, Key Topics), then contextualizing sentence naming the paper and its central thesis.

**Flow:** 4 standardized sections: (1) Paper summary, (2) Key insights with figures, (3) "Implications for Return Stacking Strategies", (4) Conclusion.

**Headers:** Thematic phrases, not dry labels: "Exploring the Longevity of Trend-Following Strategies."

**Tone:** More formal and passive. Deferential to original authors. "The authors find that..."

**Figures:** 2-3, reproduced from the original paper with "(Original: Figure 6)" notation.

**Close:** Single concluding paragraph connecting findings to return stacking. No CTA. No product mentions.

## Product Briefs (800-1,000 words + 1,000 legal)

**Opening:** Empathetic hook question: "Having a tough time sticking with your diversifying, alternative assets while equities make all-time highs?"

**Flow:** Identical rigid template across all ETFs:
1. Hook + value prop
2. "What is Return Stacking?" + fund allocation
3. Target allocation chart (stacked bar, 200% total)
4. "Why [Strategy]?" (4 bullets)
5. Drawdown performance chart + table
6. Correlation matrix
7. Growth of $100 chart
8. Practical implementation (portfolio model comparison)
9. Institutional social proof list
10. Notes, Definitions, Glossary, Risk Factors

**Tone:** Most commercial and direct. Declarative statements. Zero hedging in marketing sections; extensive legal hedging in risk factors.

**Close:** Prospectus CTA (regulatory): "This and other important information is in the prospectus, which can be obtained by calling 1-844-737-3001."

## Advisor Guides (1,600-3,200 words)

**Opening:** Situational framing of why the conversation matters for advisors right now.

**Flow:** 7-section template:
1. "Why This Conversation Matters"
2. "What Is [Strategy]?" (plain-language)
3. Key Analogy (named metaphor: shock absorber, landlord/rent, base hits)
4. "How to Talk to Clients About [Strategy]" (Q&A with quoted client objections)
5. "Quick-Reference Bullet Points" (numbered 1-5)
6. "Managing [Strategy] with Return Stacking"
7. Conclusion

**Tone:** Most conversational and empathetic. Second-person dominant. Client objections in quotes: "I don't understand how this works." Responses are informal and direct.

**Jargon:** Deliberately low. Translate everything immediately: "Carry -- also called futures yield."

**Distinctive:** The only artifact type with client objection Q&A, named analogies, and numbered quick-reference bullets.

## Market Commentary / "Why Now" (1,600-1,900 words)

**Opening:** Provocative, timely observation creating urgency: "Sound familiar?" / anchored to a specific market event.

**Flow:** Macro thesis --> Evidence --> Return stacking reframe --> Humility caveat --> Philosophical close.

**Headers:** Bold, editorial: "How to Respond Without Overreacting", "Hedge the Unknown, Without Abandoning the Known."

**Tone:** Most editorial and opinionated. Aphoristic one-liners: "Stacking is not a forecast. It's a framework." Dramatic framing (Act I / Act II). But always includes intellectual humility: "It's entirely possible that this analogy goes too far."

**Distinctive:** Event-anchored, provocative thesis, "dose of humility" section, external survey/research citations.

## Whitepapers (7,000-8,000+ words)

**Opening:** First-principles definitional treatment. Builds from fundamentals.

**Flow:** Flexible, determined by the topic. Most whitepapers benefit from: a clear statement of the problem, logical progression of analysis, data-driven evidence, and a conclusion that synthesizes without overstating. A typical structure: Table of Contents --> Definitions --> Experimental Design --> Data --> Methodology --> Results --> Scenario Analysis --> Stacking Case Studies --> Conclusion --> References --> Appendix.

**Tone:** Most formal and academic, but still accessible. Imagine presenting to a room of thoughtful advisors, not defending a dissertation. Passive voice. Heavy hedging. Mathematical notation. No metaphors, no rhetorical questions, no emotional appeals.

**Figures:** 15-30. Every claim supported by empirical results.

**Jargon:** Very high. LaTeX-style equations. Assumes quantitative finance training.

**Close:** Formal conclusion + disclaimer. No CTA, no product mentions.

## Podcast Show Notes (~2,900 words)

**Opening:** Editorial introduction naming hosts and framing the episode's relevance.

**Flow:** Overview --> Key Topics (keyword list) --> Topics Discussed (bulleted) --> Summary --> Topic-by-topic deep dives (numbered, 150-250 words each) --> Extracted direct quotes (categorized by theme).

**Tone:** Hybrid -- editorial introductions + neutral summaries + raw conversational quotes preserving the hosts' informal voice.

**Distinctive:** Zero charts. Direct quote extractions categorized by theme. Host attribution by name.

## Marketing Emails

Two categories with distinct voices and objectives.

### Type A: Brand Emails

Emails from the Return Stacked(R) brand for product announcements, event promotions, research highlights, and firm news.

- **Tone:** Professional and polished, consistent with the broader brand voice. Warmer than a whitepaper, more buttoned-up than a blog post.
- **Length:** Concise. Respect the inbox. Get to the point within the first two sentences.
- **Structure:** Clear, specific subject line (no clickbait; the audience is too sophisticated). Brief context (1-2 sentences). Core message or announcement. Single, clear call-to-action.
- **Compliance:** Product announcements and any content referencing specific Return Stacked(R) products must adhere strictly to SEC/FINRA compliance best practices.

### Type B: Sales Emails

Emails from individual salespeople, typically to schedule meetings or drive engagement with specific research.

- **Tone:** Personable and direct. Should read like a note from a knowledgeable colleague, not a mass email. First person is appropriate.
- **Length:** Short, ideally viewable without scrolling. Three to five sentences plus a CTA.
- **Structure:** Personalized opening (reference something specific to the recipient where possible). One clear value proposition. One clear call-to-action (e.g., "Would you have 15 minutes next week to discuss?").
- **Key principle:** One email, one ask. Do not bundle multiple CTAs.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Write subject lines that are specific and honest | Use ALL CAPS, excessive punctuation, or clickbait |
| Front-load the most important information | Bury the point after paragraphs of context |
| Make the CTA obvious and easy to act on | Include multiple competing calls-to-action |
| Sound like a human being wrote it | Sound like a compliance department wrote it |

## Social Media (LinkedIn)

LinkedIn is the primary social platform. Posts fall into two categories.

### Type A: Corporate Account

Posts from the Return Stacked(R) brand account promoting products, research, media appearances, and announcements.

- **Tone:** Professional and polished. These represent the firm and must adhere to compliance best practices at all times.
- **Format:** Lead with the key insight or announcement, not "We're excited to announce..." Keep text concise; link out for depth. Use line breaks for readability (walls of text die on LinkedIn). Include relevant links with brief context on why the reader should click.
- **Content types:** Research highlights, product updates, media appearances, event promotions, firm milestones.
- **Compliance:** All posts referencing products or services must be reviewed. Avoid performance claims, guarantees, or forward-looking statements.

### Type B: Corey's Personal Account

Posts from Corey sharing research, interesting findings, and personal takes on industry topics.

- **Tone:** Casual, curious, and conversational. Corey thinking out loud, sharing something interesting with peers. Not an official brand statement.
- **Format:** Hook in the first line (LinkedIn truncates after ~150 characters; earn the "see more" click). Short paragraphs or single-line statements for pacing. End with a question or observation that invites engagement. "I" and personal perspective are acceptable.
- **Content types:** Interesting research findings, contrarian observations, industry commentary, links to Return Stacked(R) blog posts and whitepapers with personal framing.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Lead with the insight, not the announcement | Start with "We're thrilled to share..." or "Excited to announce..." |
| Write a compelling first line that earns the click | Assume people will read past the fold |
| Use line breaks and white space | Write dense paragraphs |
| Share a genuine point of view | Post generic industry platitudes |

# 7. The Return Stacking Bridge

**Every piece of content** (except pure whitepapers) must contain a section that connects the topic back to return stacking. This is the corpus's most distinctive and consistent stylistic feature.

| Blog posts | Integrated throughout, culminating in "Key Takeaways" |
| Market commentary | The concluding reframe |
| Product briefs | "What is Return Stacking?" (templated) |

# 8. Analogy Construction Rules

1. **Introduce the physical analogy BEFORE the technical concept.**
2. **Analogies must be drawn from familiar domains:** real estate (apartment buildings, mortgages), insurance (producer hedging), everyday objects (shock absorbers, trampolines), nature (lions on the veldt, boulders rolling).
3. **One named analogy per advisor guide.** This becomes the guide's mnemonic anchor.
4. **Blog posts: 0-2 analogies maximum.** Don't over-metaphor analytical content.
5. **Academic reviews and whitepapers: zero analogies.**
6. **Market commentary: 1 extended narrative analogy** (two-act drama, EM parallel) that structures the piece.

---

# 9. Rhetorical Questions

Use 2-4 per article, strategically placed at:

1. **The opening** (to voice the reader's skepticism): "If diversification is so good, why don't all investors add alternatives?"
2. **Transition points** (to set up the next section): "How do we take advantage of these benefits?"
3. **The pivotal reframe** (to signal paradigm shift): "What if we didn't have to sell anything to make room?"

Rhetorical questions must be phrased as if the reader or their client is asking them. Plain language, using "we" or "you." Never cluster them.

# 10. Emotional Boundaries

- **Cool-to-warm temperature.** Never hot.
- **Controlled passion** for: democratization of institutional strategies, solving the behavior gap, the power of diversification during crises.
- **Measured calm** for: risk, loss, regulatory matters.
- **Urgency without panic** in market commentary.
- **The emotional peak** should connect abstract math to human consequences. Use restraint -- a single devastating phrase lands harder than a paragraph of alarm: "the difference between a fruitful retirement and renewing the job search."

# 11. Self-Aware Limitations

Always acknowledge when evidence is incomplete:

- "In the realm of academic factor studies, this is an incredibly limited data set."
- "No diversification strategy works perfectly every single time."
- "It's entirely possible that this analogy goes too far."
- "Macro forecasting is hard. Portfolio humility is essential."

This intellectual honesty is a core brand differentiator. It builds trust and signals the authors are not selling but teaching.

# 12. What NOT to Do

1. **Don't promise outcomes.** Never use "will" for future performance.
2. **Don't name Return Stacked ETF tickers in analytical articles.** Discuss RS products conceptually ("a fund that seeks to provide $1 of bonds and $1 of alternatives"). Benchmark/index fund tickers (e.g., VFINX, SPY) used in backtests are fine.
3. **Don't use first person singular.** The voice is always "we."
4. **Don't dismiss objections.** Steelman, concede, recontextualize.
5. **Don't use "complex" or "complicated" for return stacking.** Complexity is reserved for what return stacking simplifies.
6. **Don't skip the Return Stacking Bridge section.** Every piece connects back.
7. **Don't present data without framing.** Every chart needs an interpretive sentence before it.
8. **Don't write a blog post without Key Takeaways.** Bullet-pointed, with bold lead-ins, addressed to advisors.
9. **Don't use superlatives without qualification.** Prefer comparative forms: "more resilient" over "most resilient."
10. **Don't write marketing language that could be mistaken for a guarantee.** The line between advocacy and selling is maintained by educational framing, "we believe" hedges, and addressing the advisor (not the end client).

# 14. Advanced Patterns

## The "Bonus Section" Device

For anticipated follow-up questions or edge cases, append a "Bonus Section" after the main conclusion but before disclaimers. This section addresses the natural "but what about...?" objection by extending the analysis to a degraded or extreme scenario:

- "Bonus Section: What if our cash reserves don't earn as much as the borrow rate?"
- "Extended Example: What happens during an inverted yield curve?"

This device demonstrates thoroughness without cluttering the main argument.

## Comparison Tables for Paradigm Shifts

Market commentary and some blog posts use a distinctive two-column comparison table to crystallize the shift from conventional thinking to return stacking thinking:

| Traditional Reaction | Return Stacking Response |
|---|---|
| Sell bonds, buy gold | Stack gold on top of bonds |
| Reduce equity, hold cash | Stack alternatives on top of equity |

This format is powerful for making the "And Not Or" / "Yes And" move visual and scannable.

# 16. Disclosure Structure

Every piece of content that shows backtested, simulated, or hypothetical results must include disclosures. The structure follows a fixed sequence -- include all sections that apply, omit any that are irrelevant.

## Disclosure Sections (in order)

1. **Important Disclosures on Hypothetical Performance** -- Standard boilerplate: results are hypothetical/illustrative, indexes are unmanaged, no representation that any account will achieve similar results, sharp differences between hypothetical and actual performance, benefit of hindsight, no financial risk in backtests. Do not rewrite from scratch each time.

2. **Methodology** -- Widget/analysis-specific. How the portfolio is constructed, rebalancing approach, the explicit mathematical formula for portfolio returns, fee structure with all assumptions, simulation approach if applicable, definitions of all displayed statistics.

3. **Data Sources** -- Every index/data series by full name, exact date period, gross/net distinction, reinvestment assumptions, any special treatments (e.g. "Gold returns reflect spot price changes only and are reduced by an estimated 40 basis point annual access fee").

4. **Index Definitions** -- Only indices actually used. 1-3 sentences each: what the index measures, composition methodology, relevant details.

5. **Term Definitions (Glossary)** -- Only terms relevant to the specific piece. Concise (1-2 sentences).

6. **Third-Party Data Provider Disclosures** -- Required when using licensed index data. Include the provider's required attribution language verbatim.

7. **Important Information (Legal Boilerplate)** -- Standard block: information sourced from Newfound Research LLC and ReSolve Asset Management SEZC, forward-looking statements caveat, no assurance of performance, not investment advice, past performance disclaimer, no reproduction without permission, copyright notice.

## Disclosure Writing Principles

- **Tone:** Formal, precise, cautious. Legal/compliance language, not marketing copy.
- **Specificity over vagueness.** State exact date ranges, exact fee assumptions, exact index names. "Based on 312 months of historical returns from 12/31/1999 through 12/31/2025" beats "based on historical data."
- **Only disclose what is used.** Do not pad with irrelevant definitions.
- **Formulas are required.** If the analysis computes portfolio returns, the exact formula must appear in the methodology section.
- **The hypothetical performance and legal boilerplate sections are standardized.** Adapt only where specific context requires it.

## Disclosure Styling

- Placed in a collapsible section at the bottom, separated by `border-top: 1px solid #eee`
- Toggle: 12px, weight 600, uppercase, letter-spacing 0.5px, `#888` text
- Content: 11px, line-height 1.6, `#777`, justified
- Figure-level disclaimers sit directly beneath each figure in 11px italic `#999`

# 17. Compliance Framework

## Product-Referencing Content

Any content that references specific Return Stacked(R) products or services (certain marketing emails, corporate LinkedIn posts, product announcements) requires:

- Pre-publication compliance review
- No forward-looking performance statements
- No guarantees of returns or outcomes
- Balanced presentation of risks and potential benefits
- Required disclaimers and disclosures as determined by compliance counsel
- Fair and balanced presentation: if benefits are discussed, risks must be too

## Non-Product Content (Research and Education)

Whitepapers, blog posts, and general educational content that do not reference specific products have more flexibility but should still:

- Avoid language that could be construed as guarantees ("will," "always," "certain to")
- Use appropriate qualifying language ("historically," "may," "has tended to," "based on backtested results")
- Clearly label backtested or hypothetical results and include relevant disclaimers
- Cite data sources
- Avoid cherry-picking time periods or metrics to support a predetermined conclusion

## Language to Avoid (All Content)

| Avoid | Use Instead |
|---|---|
| "Guaranteed returns" | "Historically, this approach has..." |
| "Will outperform" | "Has the potential to improve..." |
| "Risk-free" | "Designed to help manage risk" |
| "You should invest in..." | "Advisors may want to consider..." |
| "Always works" | "Has been effective across multiple market environments" |
