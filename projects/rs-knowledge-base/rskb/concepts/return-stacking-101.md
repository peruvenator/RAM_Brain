# Return Stacking 101

## Definition

Return Stacking is the idea of **layering one investment return on top of another, achieving more than $1.00 of exposure for each $1.00 invested.**

For decades, sophisticated institutional investors have thoughtfully applied leverage to include diversifying alternative strategies without diluting their core stock and bond allocations. Today, professionally managed exchange-traded products allow investors to implement this concept in a packaged, daily-liquid wrapper.

## The $1 + $1 framework

The defining promise of every Return Stacked® product is the same:

> **For every $1 invested, the fund seeks to provide $1 of exposure to [base] AND $1 of exposure to [stack].**

The "stack" is always a diversifying return stream — managed futures, carry, merger arbitrage, gold/bitcoin, or systematic macro — designed to have low average correlation to the base.

## Three pillars of the value proposition

### 1. Pursuing diversification without sacrifice
Investors can introduce diversifying assets and strategies without sacrificing exposure to their traditional asset allocation. The diversifier comes *on top of* the core, not *in place of* it.

### 2. Potential for enhanced returns
By introducing additional sources of return, return stacking creates the potential for outperformance — particularly attractive in environments where expected returns for traditional assets may be muted.

### 3. Potential to improve diversification
By thoughtfully introducing differentiated return streams, investors may gain a diversification advantage with the potential to reduce portfolio volatility and drawdowns.

## Capital efficiency: the underlying mechanic

The base sleeve (e.g., equities or bonds) is held mostly in cash-efficient form — physical ETFs and/or index futures. The diversifying stack is then implemented via **futures contracts**, which require only *initial margin* to hold full notional exposure. Excess cash sits in Treasuries or money-market funds and earns yield. The combination delivers ~200% notional exposure ($1 base + $1 stack) per $1 of NAV.

In the U.S. ETF lineup, most futures positioning is held inside a **Cayman Subsidiary** (a "CFC") for tax-qualifying-income reasons under RIC rules. RSSB is the exception — it doesn't trade commodity futures, so it doesn't need a CFC.

## Behavioral / psychological pillar

A key advantage that doesn't always get airtime: **psychological comfort**. By delivering core exposure *first* and the diversifier *on top*, return stacking reduces the behavioral friction investors typically experience when alternatives are added in place of familiar assets. The investor doesn't feel like they "gave up" stocks or bonds to make room for trend or carry — they kept everything they already had.

This matters because most alternative strategies *eventually* go through long, painful underperformance windows. If the diversifier was added *in place of* core exposure, the investor often capitulates at the worst time. If it's *stacked on top of* core exposure, the investor stays put, because the core is still doing what they expect.

## Historical context

These concepts trace back to the **portable alpha** strategies institutional investors have used since the 1980s — large pension plans and endowments routinely apply leverage to combine a core beta with a diversifying return stream. What's new is the **packaging**: daily-liquid ETFs and mutual funds make the technique available to RIAs, advisors, and individual investors.

## Common misconceptions to push back on

- **"It's leveraged, so it's risky."** Return stacking *is* leveraged in notional terms, but the leverage is applied to *uncorrelated* return streams. The risk profile depends on how the diversifier behaves relative to the base, not just on gross notional.
- **"You're just doubling your equity bet."** No — the stack is a diversifier with low average correlation to the base. The whole point is that the *combined* portfolio behaves differently than either component alone.
- **"Why not just buy the diversifier separately?"** You can. But to do so, you have to *sell* something to fund it. Return stacking lets you keep the core *and* add the diversifier in one ticker.
